## ★ 1. Nigeria has not imported a drop of aviation fuel in 13 months. It is now exporting to Europe.

**Hook:** Nigeria ran 13 months without importing aviation fuel. In June 2026, Dangote shipped
N757 billion in jet fuel to Europe and became the continent's top external supplier for that
month.

**Context:** The Nigerian Midstream Downstream Petroleum Regulatory Authority confirmed zero
aviation fuel imports by Oil Marketing Companies from June 2025 to June 2026. Domestic
refineries, led by Dangote, now supply 95 percent of Nigeria's Jet A-1. In June, Dangote
exported 466,000 metric tonnes to Europe, overtaking the United States as the largest external
supplier to the continent that month. Exports nearly doubled from May's 232,000 metric tonnes.

**His angle:** This is what happens when production data is consolidated and the operation
runs from one source. Nigeria spent years unable to see, in real time, what refineries were
producing, storing, and shipping. The same fragmentation lives inside most Nigerian businesses:
no single view of inventory, procurement, and output. The Dangote story is not only about
refining capacity. It is about operational data that is connected well enough to manage one of
the world's most complex supply chains at scale. The angle for Bamidele's audience: what
Dangote proved at national scale, a mid-size oil and gas operator can prove at company scale.

## ★ 2. 143 companies competed for 37 oil blocks. Thirteen of the fifty blocks attracted no bids at all.

**Hook:** Nigeria's 2025 licensing round closed July 21. Thirty-one companies won 37 blocks.
Thirteen blocks drew zero interest.

**Context:** The NUPRC Commercial Bid Conference held July 21, 2026, at Transcorp Event Centre
in Abuja brought 143 companies into competition for 50 oil and gas blocks. Only 37 blocks
attracted about 200 bids. Thirty-one companies emerged as winners, covering blocks across the
Niger Delta, Benin Basin, Anambra, Chad, and Benue basins. The 13 blocks that drew no
interest will return to the licensing basket for further technical work. NUPRC warned that
winners who fail to pay their signature bonus and receive ministerial clearance within 90 days
will forfeit their award to reserve bidders.

**His angle:** Winning a block required passing commercial evaluation: signature bonus, work
programme commitment, and performance security, combined into a weighted score. Companies that
could not demonstrate that on paper did not get into the room. NUPRC's process is a version
of the same procurement gate that NCDMB is building for contractors. Nigerian oil and gas
companies now face two separate documentation requirements for two separate agencies. The
companies with organised financial and operational records will navigate both; the ones without
them will miss both windows.

## ★ 3. Nigerian banks are lending at 33.16 percent. At that rate, operational waste is a funded cost.

**Hook:** The CBN held the policy rate at 26.5 percent on July 21 for the second consecutive
meeting. Banks' maximum lending rate sits at 33.16 percent as of July 26.

**Context:** The Monetary Policy Committee met July 20 to 21, 2026, and voted unanimously to
retain the MPR at 26.5 percent, citing heightened global geopolitical risk and the need to
consolidate inflation moderation and naira stability. The Cash Reserve Ratio for deposit money
banks stays at 45 percent. ThisDay reported on July 26 that the maximum bank lending rate
eased slightly to 33.16 percent.

**His angle:** A business borrowing at 33 percent per year cannot afford to lose weeks to
reconciliation, manual data entry, or decisions made on stale reports. Operational
consolidation is not a technology story at this cost of capital. It is a maths problem. Every
hour of manual reconciliation, every missed invoice, every procurement decision made without
current inventory data is costing more than it did at 15 percent. The CBN is not moving that
rate down anytime soon. The businesses that survive this cycle will be the ones that cut
internal friction before it compounds into a borrowing problem.

## 4. A startup built a control layer that sits between AI agents and everything they touch.

**Hook:** Enterprises are deploying AI agents into production with no way to see what those
agents are actually doing. Alterion launched Draco on July 16 to fix that.

**Context:** Alterion announced Draco on July 16, 2026: a runtime control plane that intercepts
every prompt and action from an AI agent before it reaches infrastructure, models agent
behavior in real time, applies programmable guardrails, and blocks high-risk actions before
they execute. The platform requires no code changes to existing agents. Draco targets
governance gaps under SOC 2, ISO 42001, and the EU AI Act, giving security and compliance
teams visibility into agent intent, not only logs after the fact. Alterion was founded by a
former McKinsey Partner and a former Google Engineering VP.

**His angle:** The Gartner finding from May 2026 (40 percent of production AI agents will be
shut down by 2027) is a governance failure. Draco is the architecture that prevents it. What
Draco does for AI agents is what a well-built operations system does for procurement: one
place where the activity is visible, the rules are enforced, and the audit trail is automatic.
Companies that deploy agents on top of fragmented operations will need this layer. Companies
that build one integrated system first will implement it faster because the infrastructure
underneath is already clean.

## 5. Nigeria's Senate just repealed the 1997 law that governed its entire insurance industry.

**Hook:** The Insurance Regulatory Commission Bill passed the Senate on July 21. The 1997
NAICOM Act is gone.

**Context:** Nigeria's Senate voted on July 21, 2026, to repeal the National Insurance
Commission Act of 1997 and replace it with the Insurance Regulatory Commission Bill, 2025.
The new law renames the regulator the Insurance Regulatory Commission, grants it greater
operational independence, stronger enforcement powers, and authority to collaborate with
international regulators. The existing 1997 framework was judged too outdated to reflect
modern industry realities or global regulatory standards. The bill now moves to the House of
Representatives before presidential assent.

**His angle:** Nigerian businesses holding asset insurance, trade credit insurance, or
contractor liability cover should track this closely. A new legal framework means new minimum
requirements, new documentation standards, and potentially new mandatory covers. For oil and
gas procurement specifically, contractor insurance is part of the qualification pack for both
NCDMB and NUPRC processes. The companies that manage compliance records in one place will
know exactly what needs to update when the law changes. The ones working from folders and
email threads will spend months catching up.

## 6. The IMF says Nigerian SMEs have shifted to stablecoins for imports. $59 billion moved through crypto in one year.

**Hook:** Nigerian small businesses are settling import invoices in USDT because the official
FX system cannot clear fast enough. The IMF documented it in a June 2026 paper.

**Context:** An IMF study published June 16, 2026, found that Nigerian SMEs in electronics,
auto parts, and textiles have switched to dollar-pegged stablecoins for cross-border trade
because official channels involve multi-day clearing delays and foreign exchange liquidity
gaps. Nigeria accounts for about 60 percent of sub-Saharan Africa's stablecoin inflows since
2019. Between July 2023 and June 2024, approximately $59 billion in crypto inflows moved into
Nigeria. The IMF flags that widespread stablecoin adoption is creating digital dollarisation
risk that weakens the naira's monetary policy transmission.

**His angle:** This is a workaround that became infrastructure. Nigerian businesses did not
choose stablecoins for ideological reasons. They chose them because the alternative was a
missed shipment. The FX system's fragmentation pushed them to build a parallel one. That
pattern appears inside businesses too: when the official internal process is too slow or too
opaque, people build shadow systems (a personal spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, a manual
tracker). The workaround survives until a regulator notices, a staff member leaves, or an
audit arrives. The fix is the same in both cases: one connected system where the real data is
visible in real time.
